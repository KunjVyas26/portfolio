01 The real problem

The ask was "better task management." The problem was losing 45 minutes a day just getting oriented.

The brief: redesign a B2B workspace tool for teams managing 10+ concurrent projects. The existing product had tasks, timelines, and dashboards. Usage data told a different story — users opened the app an average of 11 times a day, but completed meaningful work in fewer than 4 of those sessions. The other 7 were orientation: finding where something was, checking what someone else had done, or trying to remember what they'd been working on before a meeting interrupted them.

The problem wasn't task management. It was context loss — the cost of switching between projects and having to rebuild your mental model every single time.

"I spend more time figuring out what I should be doing than actually doing it. Every morning I open five tabs just to remember where I left off yesterday."

User interview · Senior PM, 14-person product team, 12 active projects

The reframe: don't build a better task list — build a faster way back to context. Every surface decision followed from that.

02 Real constraints

B2B constraints are different. The buyer and the user are rarely the same person.

Existing data model couldn't change — hard engineering constraint
The backend modeled tasks under projects under workspaces in a strict hierarchy. Users didn't think hierarchically. I had to create a UI that felt flat and associative while the data stayed nested. Every cross-project view had to work within that constraint.
8 different team personas, all in the same product
PMs, engineers, designers, QA, and executives. A dashboard useful to a PM was noise to an engineer. Role-based defaults were required, but couldn't be mandated — admins resisted anything that "forced" behavior on their teams.
Migration risk — 3,000+ active users couldn't be disrupted
Every structural change had to be phased, with escape hatches back to old patterns. I designed a side-by-side "classic / new" toggle that ran for 8 weeks post-launch to reduce churn risk.
Design system was non-existent — had to be built in parallel
No shared component library. Engineers and designers each made their own button. 48 unique CTA styles found via FullStory. Building the system wasn't a separate workstream — it was a prerequisite for shipping anything consistent.
03 What research actually found

Four findings. The most important one came from FullStory, not interviews.

11×
Average daily app opens per user. Meaningful work sessions: fewer than 4. The other 7 opens were orientation — finding context, not doing work.
48
Unique CTA button styles found via FullStory click analysis. Not 48 use cases — 48 inconsistent implementations of the same actions. Users had learned to ignore half of them.
73%
Of cross-project status checks were done by navigating to the home dashboard — not the project sidebar. The sidebar navigation model was wrong for the most common workflow.
2 min
Average time to re-orient after a calendar interruption. Users called it "the restart tax." Every designer on the team recognised it immediately from their own experience.

The FullStory data was the most valuable input. Users had developed workarounds that revealed exactly where the IA had failed them — browser bookmarks to specific project states, duplicate tabs kept open as "memory," and comments left on tasks as personal notes to their future selves.

04 How I worked

14 weeks. One architectural pivot. A design system built from necessity, not process.

Wk 1–3
Audit + behavioral research
FullStory session review (40 sessions), 14 user interviews across 5 roles, competitor teardown of Linear, Notion, Asana, and Height. Mapped "the restart tax" as the core problem.
Wk 4–5
IA redesign — attempt 1: activity feed model
Designed a Twitter-style activity feed as the home surface. Every change across all projects surfaced chronologically. Tested with 8 users. Overwhelming — too much noise, no priority signal.
Pivoted
Wk 6–8
Focus View + context resume model
Built around "where you were" not "what happened." Home surface shows last 3 active projects with a resumable context snapshot. 9/10 users found their work within 20 seconds.
Shipped direction
Wk 9–11
Design system — 48 components
Audited all 48 CTA variants. Consolidated to 3 button types. Built full component library with usage docs, states, and accessibility specs. Ran team training before handoff.
System shipped
Wk 12–14
Migration design + classic toggle + QA
Designed the classic/new toggle and migration onboarding. Cut scope: removed AI-suggested task prioritization — no baseline data to validate suggestions against. Shipped clean.
1 feature deferred
05 The decisions that mattered

Three choices that defined the product. And why I made them.

1
"Where you were" over "what happened" as the home surface organizing principle
Why
The activity feed (attempt 1) showed everything that changed. It was accurate — and completely useless for getting back to work. Users needed to answer one question: "where was I?" not "what happened while I was gone?" The Focus View answers in under 20 seconds: last open task, next blocker, last person who touched it. 9/10 users chose it over the activity feed for morning orientation.
What I killed: The "All Updates" inbox pattern. Requested by 4 of 14 interviewees — but those 4 were all managers checking on others' work, not individual contributors doing their own. I built a separate team-view for that persona rather than making the home surface serve both equally badly.
2
Build the design system before any new features — even when it delayed visible progress
Why
48 unique CTA implementations meant users had learned to ignore roughly half of all action buttons in the product. Consistency isn't aesthetic — it's functional. A button that looks like a button gets clicked. Spending weeks 9–11 on the system instead of new features narrowed scope. Everything that shipped was consistent, predictable, and accessible from day one. CTA visibility scores increased 41%.
The resistance I faced: Engineering pushed back — it felt like slowing down for paperwork. I made the case with the FullStory data: 48 button styles was an engineering maintenance cost, not just a design problem. Once framed as technical debt reduction, the system work was approved without further negotiation.
3
Classic/new toggle at launch instead of hard cutover — even though it doubled QA scope
Why
3,000+ active users with established workflows is a real migration risk. A hard cutover with no rollback has killed products. The toggle gave users control: try new, switch back if needed, decide on your own timeline. It ran for 8 weeks. By week 4, 71% of users had permanently switched — without being forced. By week 8, the classic toggle was removed. No support tickets. No churn spike.
What this cost: Every feature had to be built and tested in both interface states. QA scope doubled for 8 weeks. The right cost to pay — forced migration on a B2B product without proof the new experience is better is a trust violation you can't undo.
06 Impact

What moved at launch — and the assumption I'm still not sure about.

−35%
Context-switch time
Measured as time to locate and resume an active task after navigating away. Tested in timed usability sessions across 3 simultaneous project contexts.
+41%
CTA visibility
Measured via first-click testing in Maze. Consolidating 48 variants down to 3 button types produced the single largest improvement in task-start rate.
71%
Voluntary new UI adoption at week 4
Without coercion. Users chose the new interface. This is the migration metric that mattered most — adoption by choice is the cleanest signal that the redesign was genuinely better.
−32%
Cross-project status check time
Focus View + team overview reduced the time to answer "what is the status of all my projects?" — the single most common executive use case in the product.

What I'm still not sure about: Whether Focus View helps over months, not weeks. The "restart tax" is a daily problem — but as users build new habits, do they need the resumable context surface as much, or does it become scaffolding they outgrow? That's the question I'd instrument and answer in Q2 post-launch.

07 Reflection

What I learned. What I'd do differently.

What this project taught me
In B2B, the most valuable research data is behavioral, not attitudinal. What users told me in interviews and what FullStory showed they actually did were different enough to completely change the design direction. Interview data got me to the activity feed. Behavioral data killed it. Always instrument before you interview — the gap between stated and actual behavior is where the real design problem lives.
What I'd change
I'd push for the design system in week 1, not week 9. I built it mid-project because the inconsistency became impossible to ignore. An upfront audit would have revealed the same problem faster and given me leverage to negotiate the timeline before scope was committed. In any B2B redesign, the design system should be workstream 1, not a response to the chaos of workstream 3.
